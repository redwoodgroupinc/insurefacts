Compulsory third party insurance, usually called CTP, is a type of insurance that every registered vehicle in Australia must have. It is often confused with car insurance in general, but it covers something quite specific: injuries to people. This article explains what CTP covers, what it doesn’t, and how it works in each state and territory.

## What CTP covers

CTP insurance pays for people who are injured or killed in a motor vehicle crash caused by the driver of the insured vehicle. Depending on the scheme, this can include medical treatment, rehabilitation and compensation for injured people. If someone dies in a crash, it can also cover funeral costs and compensation for their dependants. [[1]](#source-1)

All states and territories have CTP schemes, which are funded by compulsory premiums on all registered motor vehicles. [[8]](#source-8)

## What CTP doesn’t cover

CTP doesn’t pay for damage to vehicles or other property. Those costs are covered by other types of car insurance, such as third party property damage or comprehensive insurance, which are optional. [[1]](#source-1) [[3]](#source-3)

For example, Moneysmart explains that if you damage someone else’s car and you only have CTP, you have to pay for that damage yourself. [[3]](#source-3)

## How CTP works in each state and territory

Each state and territory runs its own CTP scheme, with its own rules and regulator. How you pay for CTP also differs.

| State or territory | How CTP is paid for | Scheme or regulator |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New South Wales | A Green Slip bought from a licensed CTP insurer [[4]](#source-4) | State Insurance Regulatory Authority (SIRA) |
| Queensland | Paid with vehicle registration [[13]](#source-13) | Motor Accident Insurance Commission (MAIC) |
| Victoria | The transport accident charge, shown on the registration renewal notice [[6]](#source-6) | Transport Accident Commission (TAC) |
| Western Australia | Included when you pay vehicle registration (the vehicle licence) [[7]](#source-7) | Insurance Commission of Western Australia |
| South Australia | Paid at the same time as vehicle registration [[9]](#source-9) | CTP Insurance Regulator |
| Australian Capital Territory | Paid with vehicle registration [[10]](#source-10) | ACT Government |
| Tasmania | Run by the Motor Accidents Insurance Board [[8]](#source-8) | Motor Accidents Insurance Board (MAIB) |
| Northern Territory | Part of the registration fee [[11]](#source-11) | Northern Territory Government |

In New South Wales, there are several licensed CTP insurers, and the price of a Green Slip depends partly on individual risk factors. [[4]](#source-4) In the Northern Territory, CTP must be paid with registration even if the owner also has comprehensive insurance from a private insurer. [[11]](#source-11)

## If the injured person caused the crash

What happens when the injured person was at fault depends on the state or territory:

- **Queensland:** if you are injured in a crash that you entirely caused, or where no one was at fault, the CTP insurer is unlikely to accept your claim. [[2]](#source-2)
- **New South Wales:** people who were wholly or mostly at fault can receive statutory benefits, but weekly payments are limited to 52 weeks. People who weren’t at fault and whose injuries are more than “threshold injuries” can receive income benefits for longer. [[5]](#source-5)

The rules in other states and territories differ, so the relevant scheme’s website is the place to check.

## Complaints and disputes

The Australian Financial Complaints Authority (AFCA) generally cannot consider complaints about CTP insurance. [[12]](#source-12) Disputes about CTP claims are handled under each state or territory’s own scheme, and the scheme regulators listed above publish information about their processes.

## Where to check the details

CTP rules, benefits and prices are set under state and territory laws and can change. The regulator or scheme for your state or territory, listed in the table above, has the current rules.
