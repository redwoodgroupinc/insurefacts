Canada’s public health insurance is run by the provinces and territories. Each plan is designed mainly to cover care in your home province or territory. When you travel, whether to another part of Canada or abroad, your plan may pay for only part of your medical care, or none of it. This article explains how coverage works in each case, based on Government of Canada guidance.

## Medical care outside Canada

The Government of Canada warns travellers that their provincial or territorial health plan may cover nothing, or only a very small portion, of the costs of medical care abroad, and that it never pays up front. [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2)

When a provincial plan does pay for emergency care received outside Canada, it generally pays based on the amount it would have paid for similar services in the home province or territory. The traveller is responsible for the rest of the bill. [[3]](#source-3) The Government of Canada also notes that a medical evacuation can cost more than $50,000. [[1]](#source-1)

Provincial plans may also not cover the costs of complications from medical procedures done abroad, and most travel insurance policies won’t cover planned medical procedures in another country. [[5]](#source-5)

## Medical care in another province or territory

Under the Canada Health Act, provincial and territorial plans must meet a “portability” criterion. This allows residents who are temporarily away from home, for example on business or vacation, to receive medically necessary hospital and physician services for urgent or emergency needs. It doesn’t entitle residents to elective (non-urgent) care, or shorter wait times, in another province. [[4]](#source-4)

Provinces and territories have agreements that generally allow your health card to be accepted, instead of payment, for insured hospital and physician services in another province or territory. Quebec is the only province that does not take part in the reciprocal billing agreement for physician services. [[4]](#source-4)

Some costs fall outside these arrangements, including prescription drugs and ambulance services. These services may be heavily subsidized for residents of a province but not for visitors, so a traveller from another province may have to pay for them. [[4]](#source-4)

## What travel medical insurance can cover

Travel health insurance is bought from private insurers and can help pay for emergency medical care while travelling. Some policies also cover trip cancellation or interruption. What a policy pays for, and how much, depends on its terms. [[1]](#source-1)

The Government of Canada advises travellers to read their policy before they leave, including any deductibles and the destinations and activities it excludes. [[2]](#source-2)

## Pre-existing conditions

Travel insurance policies can limit or exclude cover for pre-existing medical conditions. The Government of Canada’s guidance highlights that:

- travellers need to check how their policy defines and limits pre-existing conditions
- some policies include a “stability clause”, which requires that your medical condition hasn’t changed for a set period before the trip
- if pre-existing conditions aren’t fully disclosed on the insurer’s form, the insurer may refuse to pay medical bills [[2]](#source-2) [[6]](#source-6)

## Where to check the details

Each province and territory sets its own rules for out-of-province and out-of-country coverage, including how long residents can be away and still stay covered. Your provincial or territorial health plan can confirm what it pays for, and Travel.gc.ca has the Government of Canada’s current advice for travellers. [[1]](#source-1)
